\subsection{Manual cross-validation}
\label{subsec:results-crossval}
Of the 30 authors sampled under the scheme described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:methods-crossval}, Table~\ref{tab:crossval} summarizes agreement between OpenAlex's \texttt{h\_index} and the corresponding value I located by hand in Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar. Coverage itself varies sharply: Scopus returned a value for 26 of the 30 authors, Google Scholar for 19, and Web of Science for 16.

Scopus tracks OpenAlex most closely, both in coverage and in magnitude of disagreement. Google Scholar is systematically higher than OpenAlex, consistent with its broader crawl of preprints, theses, and other grey literature and its comparative permissiveness toward self-citation; this ordering (Google Scholar $>$ Scopus $>$ OpenAlex $>$ Web of Science) reproduces cleanly among the six global top-h\textsubscript{1} sanity-check authors, none of whom show a discrepancy exceeding 27\% of their OpenAlex value in either direction.

Four authors, however, show disagreement well outside this range. I read this as a signature of author-disambiguation failure (multiple distinct people merged under one OpenAlex author ID) rather than of genuine cross-database disagreement:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Donald Small} (Johns Hopkins): $\mathrm{h}_1^{\mathrm{OpenAlex}}=103$ against Scopus $=57$ and Google Scholar $=59$.
	\item \textbf{Karen S.\ Calhoun} (University of Michigan): $\mathrm{h}_1^{\mathrm{OpenAlex}}=45$ against Scopus $=11$ and Google Scholar $=13$.
	\item \textbf{Lei Jiang} (University of Science and Technology of China): $\mathrm{h}_1^{\mathrm{OpenAlex}}=215$ against Web of Science $=115$, consistent with documented China-specific coverage discrepancies in OpenAlex~\citep{zheng2025}.
	\item \textbf{A.\ Jafari} (Sharif University of Technology): $\mathrm{h}_1^{\mathrm{OpenAlex}}=139$, with Scopus $=133$ and Google Scholar $=224$; Web of Science reports only $41$.
\end{itemize}
\textcolor{red}{The first three cases show large OpenAlex--Scopus discrepancies (factors of 1.8--4.1$\times$) consistent with multi-person merges. The A.\ Jafari case is different: OpenAlex (139) and Scopus (133) agree closely (4\% difference), and the low Web of Science value (41) is more plausibly explained by WoS's documented undercoverage of non-Anglophone institutions than by an OpenAlex over-merge. Cross-database h-index differences alone cannot distinguish disambiguation errors from coverage differences; publication-level identity verification (matching individual works across databases) would be needed for a definitive conclusion and is beyond the scope of this paper. We retain A.\ Jafari in the perturbation ratio pool as a conservative choice but note that this case is less clearly a disambiguation failure than the other three.}
The \textcolor{red}{three more definitive} cases fall in the non-Anglophone-institution or common-name strata of the sample, matching the concern raised in Subsection~\ref{subsec:methods-crossval} that disambiguation is more error-prone for exactly these authors. Because such merges can only inflate an author's apparent $\mathrm{h}_1$, and $\mathrm{h}_1$ feeds directly into $\mathrm{h}_2$ and $\mathrm{h}_3$, a handful of merged identities at the right institution could measurably distort an institution- or country-level ranking; I flag this as an open limitation rather than one this sample can size definitively. This sample is deliberately adversarial, chosen to find errors, not to estimate how often they occur. The 4/30 (13\%) outlier fraction should not be read as an estimate of how much of the full dataset is affected. The same pattern, disambiguation precision concentrated lower for common names and non-Anglophone institutions, appears in an independent benchmark of 2{,}700 scholars sampled systematically across three regions~\citep{zhao2025}, corroborating that this is a geographically and onomastically concentrated failure mode rather than an artifact of this particular sample. OpenAlex's maintainers report an ongoing correction effort targeting exactly this kind of over-merging, with corresponding-author identification precision rising from 0.60 to 0.92 (F1 0.72 to 0.90) after a 2026 rework of the underlying author-matching model~\citep{demes2026}. Subsection~\ref{subsec:results-sensitivity} quantifies how much a population-wide error rate at or below the adversarial sample's raw rate would actually move this paper's rankings.

The four Arts and Humanities threshold authors sampled (Gillian Ramchand and G.\ Herbert Fowler at Oxford; D.H.\ Mellor and James Raven at Cambridge) illustrate the range of under-coverage across major citation databases: Ramchand and Fowler returned no value in any of the three external sources, while Mellor (OA~$\mathrm{h}_1=28$) and Raven (OA~$\mathrm{h}_1=24$) appeared in Scopus at roughly half their OpenAlex values ($\mathrm{h}_1=13$ and 12, respectively); for Raven, Google Scholar (26) brackets the OpenAlex value closely. This is consistent with the well-documented weaker coverage of book-based humanities scholarship in all major citation databases~\citep{nederhof2006} and suggests that h-index comparisons in these fields should be read with correspondingly less confidence than in the sciences.

\subsection{Robustness of field-specific rankings to multi-field assignment}
\label{subsec:results-field-robustness}
The single-field assignment described in Subsection~\ref{subsec:author-assignment} asks which field accounts for the plurality of an author's publication volume, and assigns them exclusively there. To test how much this choice moves the field-level rankings, I re-run the field-specific h\textsubscript{2} computation after allowing each author to count in every field for which their OpenAlex topic-weight share is at least $\tau$ in accordance with Subsection~\ref{subsec:methods-field-robustness}.

At $\tau = 33\%$ (an author must contribute at least one third of their output to count in a field), the (institution, field) pair count rises by 16.0\% (229{,}309 $\to$ 266{,}002 pairs). The Spearman rank correlation between original and multi-field h\textsubscript{2} values, computed on the inner join of shared pairs, is $\rho = 0.960$: field rankings are highly stable. Of the 28 fields' leading institutions (26 fields, but Social Sciences and Business, Management and Accounting each have two institutions tied for the top h\textsubscript{2} value, yielding 28 leader entries), 23 are unchanged. The Wageningen Ag\&Bio leadership (Table~\ref{tab:field-leaders}) survives, Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead survives (tied with MIT, $\mathrm{h}_2 = 56$), and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences (UCAS) Environmental Science lead ($\mathrm{h}_2 = 62$, baseline $\mathrm{h}_2 = 60$) also survives. The two methods answer different questions. The single-field assignment identifies institutions where Environmental Science is researchers' \emph{primary} field; the multi-field assignment identifies institutions with the most researchers for whom it is a \emph{substantial} field.

At $\tau = 20\%$ (a more aggressive threshold admitting all authors with one fifth or more of their output in a field), the pair count rises by 39.6\% (229{,}309 to 320{,}067) and $\rho = 0.901$; 21 of 28 field leaders survive. Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead also falls at this threshold, with Stanford leading instead ($\mathrm{h}_2 = 62$), consistent with the same mechanism of Stanford having a larger population of researchers with at least 20\% CS output, even if CS is not their modal field. These findings confirm that the overall field ranking structure is broadly stable under multi-field assignment but that the field-leadership claims for smaller, highly specialised institutions depend on how narrowly ``counts in this field'' is defined.

The no-rollup and share-of-all variants are nearly indistinguishable from the default: institution h\textsubscript{2} Spearman correlations of 0.998--0.999 and top-10 overlaps of 9/10 confirm that neither the rollup rule nor the denominator choice for the 10\% share threshold materially shifts the rankings.

The most-recent-snapshot variant, which reverts to reading each author's primary institution directly from the OpenAlex author snapshot rather than from the works-based five-year window, produces a lower institution h\textsubscript{2} Spearman correlation of 0.907. Seven of the ten top-ranked institutions are shared with the default top-10, but the three that differ include institutions whose apparent performance under the most-recent-snapshot rule reflects single-paper secondary affiliations rather than sustained research presence, the pattern the primary-institution rule was designed to exclude. Country h\textsubscript{3} is more stable ($\rho = 0.991$, 9/10 top-10 overlap), consistent with country aggregation smoothing institution-level noise. These results confirm that the choice between snapshot-based and works-based attribution is the dominant source of sensitivity in this paper's rankings, while choices within the works-based approach (rollup, denominator) are comparatively minor.

Rather than assigning each author to a single institution, the \textcolor{red}{full-counting variant} credits each author's full h\textsubscript{1} to every institution meeting both thresholds in their five-year window. Of the \textcolor{red}{15.9 million authors with a works-based primary institution assigned, 18.0\%} (2,858,984) qualify at two or more institutions under this rule, producing 17.2 million (author, institution) attributions. Institution h\textsubscript{2} Spearman correlation is $\rho = 0.939$ (7/10 top-10 overlap); country h\textsubscript{3} is $\rho = 0.995$ (9/10 top-10 overlap). The three institutions entering the \textcolor{red}{full-counting} top-10 that were not in the default top-10 (the University of Adelaide, Universit\'{e} Paris-Saclay, and Universit\'{e} Paris Cit\'{e}) are large research universities whose researchers commonly hold dual appointments with national research agencies \citep{cnrsHome,insermHome}, so they gain more authors under a \textcolor{red}{full-counting} rule. The overall structure of the rankings is broadly stable at both the institution and country level.

\subsection{\textcolor{red}{Institution size-adjusted index}}
\label{subsec:h2-efficiency}

\subsubsection*{Model validation}
Figure~\ref{fig:h2-vs-size} plots institution-level $\mathrm{h}_2$ against the Egghe-predicted value $\mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1}$ for all institutions with at least 10 OpenAlex authors. A no-intercept regression, appropriate because $N = 0$ authors forces $\mathrm{h}_2 = 0$, gives slope $c_2 = 2.422$ and uncentered $R^2 = 0.909$ \textcolor{red}{(centered $R^2 = 0.810$)}: \textcolor{red}{the Egghe functional form $\mathrm{h}_2 \propto \mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1}$ accounts for the large majority of variation in h\textsubscript{2} across institutions.} The size-adjusted index $\varepsilon_2 = \mathrm{h}_2 / (c_2\,\mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1})$ captures each institution's deviation from this empirical trend\textcolor{red}{; $\varepsilon_2 > 1$ indicates above-average performance relative to the fitted regression line, and $\varepsilon_2 < 1$ below-average.}

\textcolor{red}{As a cross-check, an OLS log-log regression of $\log(\mathrm{h}_2)$ on $\log(S)$ gives slope $b = 0.404$ (implying $\beta \approx 2.47$, centered $R^2 = 0.795$), versus Egghe's $\beta_1 = 2.991$ (implied slope $0.334$), meaning the Egghe-based normalization slightly under-adjusts for size.}

\textcolor{red}{The tail-fit diagnostics (Section~\ref{subsec:methods-efficiency}) are: for $\beta_1$, $x_{\min} = 18$, $n_\text{tail} = 199{,}953$ (2M-point subsample of 13.3M), KS $D = 0.063$, SE($\beta_1$) $\approx 0.004$; a loglikelihood-ratio test comparing power-law vs.\ exponential within the tail gives $R = -25.3$ ($p \approx 0$), favouring the exponential in the extreme upper tail ($\mathrm{h}_1 \geq 18$). For $\beta_2$: $x_{\min} = 18$, $n_\text{tail} = 3{,}918$, KS $D = 0.064$, SE($\beta_2$) $\approx 0.030$; LRT $R = -9.0$ ($p \approx 0$), again favouring the exponential above $\mathrm{h}_2 = 18$. These LRT results indicate that within the extreme upper tail the exponential fits marginally better than a strict power law, while the regression evidence above shows that the power-law functional form for size-scaling is empirically well-supported across the full institution-size range. Institutions with $\mathrm{h}_2 < 18$ lie outside the fitted tail domain.}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/h2_vs_size.png}
\caption{Institution-level h\textsubscript{2} versus $\mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1}$ (Egghe's predicted scaling, $\beta_1 = 2.991$). No-intercept regression: $\mathrm{h}_2 = 2.422\,\mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1}$, uncentered $R^2 = 0.909$ \textcolor{red}{(centered $R^2 = 0.810$)}.}
\label{fig:h2-vs-size}
\end{figure}

The cross-check on Egghe's compounding structure fails sharply, however. Egghe's derivation requires that the Lotka tail exponent of the h\textsubscript{1} distribution equals the product $\alpha_0 \alpha_1$; these separate fits give $\alpha_0 = 2.454$ and $\alpha_1 = 2.994$, whose product is $7.35$, against the directly-measured $\beta_1 = 2.991$: a discrepancy of $146\%$. The analogous prediction at the next level ($\beta_2 = \beta_1 \alpha_2$) is similarly violated: $2.991 \times 1.987 = 5.944$ against the directly-measured $\beta_2 = 2.847$ ($109\%$ discrepancy).

The failure of the compounding formula does not, however, undermine the size-adjusted index. The two claims play different roles. The compounding formula ($\beta_1 = \alpha_0\alpha_1$) is a \emph{theoretical prediction} about the relationship between separately-fitted exponents; it is tested here and found wanting. The power-law scaling $\mathrm{h}_2 \propto \mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1}$ is an \emph{empirical description} (uncentered $R^2 = 0.909$\textcolor{red}{, centered $R^2 = 0.810$}) that does not depend on the compounding formula being correct. $\beta_1$ is estimated directly from the distribution of $\mathrm{h}_1$ values across authors, not derived from $\alpha_0\alpha_1$, so the 146\% discrepancy in the compounding formula has no bearing on the exponent actually used in $\varepsilon_2$. \textcolor{red}{The key assumption linking the pooled $\beta_1$ to each institution's threshold is that institutional author rosters are approximately random draws from the population-wide h\textsubscript{1} distribution. If institutions instead selectively recruit high-h\textsubscript{1} talent, this assumption is violated; the fitted amplitude $c_2 = 2.422$ and the residual size association documented below are consistent with such quality-sorting, though they do not establish it as the cause.}

\subsubsection*{\textcolor{red}{Size-adjusted} rankings}
The \textcolor{red}{size-adjusted} index $\varepsilon_2 = \mathrm{h}_2 / (c_2\,\mathrm{S}^{1/\beta_1})$ addresses Mryglod et al.'s~\citeyearpar{mryglod2022} concern: the Egghe functional form accounts for the large majority of variation in $\mathrm{h}_2$, and dividing it out isolates the residual variation not explained by headcount alone. \textcolor{red}{However, a regression of $\log(\varepsilon_2)$ on $\log(S)$ across institutions with $\geq 100$ authors ($n = 8{,}118$) yields slope $= 0.073$, $R^2 = 0.062$: a small but statistically significant positive residual association with size remains after normalization. Larger institutions still score slightly higher on $\varepsilon_2$ than the regression line predicts. The normalization substantially reduces but does not eliminate the size association; results should be read with this caveat, particularly for comparisons between very small and very large institutions. Field composition and researcher career age are additional uncontrolled confounds. A regression of field-specific $\varepsilon_2$ would be the appropriate next step and is left as future work. Career age is not available in the OpenAlex snapshot columns used here and is likewise acknowledged as a limitation.}

\subsection{Sensitivity to disambiguation error}
\label{subsec:results-sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reports, for seven simulated population-wide error rates spanning $p=1\%$ to a deliberately implausible $p=80\%$ (Subsection~\ref{subsec:methods-sensitivity}), the Spearman rank correlation and top-set overlap between the perturbed and unperturbed rankings, averaged over 50 trials, together with how often the paper's three headline field-leadership claims survive the perturbation intact. The cross-validation sample's own raw outlier fraction (4/30 $\approx 13\%$ across both waves, Subsection~\ref{subsec:results-crossval}) is included as an extra row for reference, though it carries no special status in this analysis.

Institution- and country-level rank correlations decline gradually with $p$: Spearman $\rho$(h\textsubscript{2}) falls from 0.9995 at $p=1\%$ to 0.9793 at $p=80\%$; $\rho$(h\textsubscript{3}) from 0.9998 to 0.9946. This follows from how h\textsubscript{2} and h\textsubscript{3} are computed: each is an order statistic, so what matters for a given institution or country is whether a noisy author sits near its own threshold, not how many noisy authors exist elsewhere; and because the perturbation is applied simultaneously to all institutions, individual rank shifts are dampened at the aggregate level. Top-20 institution overlap follows a U-shaped pattern: 95\% at $p=1\%$, declining to a minimum of 44\% near $p=20\%$, then recovering to 83\% at $p=80\%$. This reflects a cancellation effect: at very high error rates, roughly equal numbers of inflation and deflation events accumulate within every institution's author pool, partially restoring the pre-perturbation rank ordering among the top-ranked set.

The field-specific leadership claims differ sharply in sensitivity. UCAS's Environmental Science lead survives all 50 trials at every error rate tested. Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead is robust at $p \leq 5\%$ (48/50 trials) but displaced at $p=10\%$ (6/50) and $p=13\%$ (1/50), recovering partially at $p=80\%$ (16/50) as the cancellation effect sets in. Wageningen's Agricultural and Biological Sciences lead is the most sensitive: it holds 50/50 at $p=1\%$ but falls to 36/50 at $p=5\%$, and to 0/50 at $p=10\%$ through $p=40\%$, with partial recovery at $p=80\%$ (1/50). Wageningen's greater sensitivity relative to CMU is consistent with the country-stratified error model: several of Wageningen's main competitors in Agricultural and Biological Sciences are Chinese institutions, which receive 3$\times$ the base error rate and therefore a larger expected perturbation.
% Italy and Japan remain near-parity in h\textsubscript{3} at plausible error rates ($p \leq 5\%$; both approximately 45); at higher rates, absolute h\textsubscript{3} values increase due to the AM-GM inequality in the bidirectional model ($\tfrac{1}{2}(r + r^{-1}) > 1$ for $r \neq 1$), with Japan's larger institution pool amplifying this upward drift more.

\textcolor{red}{The high Spearman correlation coexisting with low top-set retention ($p = 13\%$, $\rho(\mathrm{h}_2) = 0.9946$ but only 47\% of the top-20 institutions are retained) is not contradictory: the Spearman correlation is dominated by thousands of institutions far from the top-20, where perturbations cause small rank shifts. The top-20 is populated by institutions often separated by only 1--3 h\textsubscript{2} points, making headline rankings sensitive to exactly the threshold-straddling errors the Monte Carlo applies.}

Institution and country rank correlations remain high across all tested rates ($\rho$(h\textsubscript{2})~$\geq 0.979$; $\rho$(h\textsubscript{3})~$\geq 0.994$), and the UCAS Environmental Science claim is similarly stable. The Wageningen Agricultural and Biological Sciences and Carnegie Mellon Computer Science field-leadership claims are sensitive above $p \approx 5\%$ and should be read with correspondingly greater caution as single-institution results. The overall finding is that h\textsubscript{2} and h\textsubscript{3} rankings are structurally determined at population scale. Individual author errors are absorbed across many threshold comparisons, and at very high error rates the bidirectional perturbations increasingly cancel within institutions.

\textcolor{red}{The three additional scenarios from Subsection~\ref{subsec:methods-sensitivity} test the robustness of this picture. Under \emph{directional inflation-only}, the absence of cancellation makes moderate error rates more destructive: top-20 institution overlap falls to 55\% at $p=5\%$ (versus 77\% bidirectional) and Wageningen's Ag\&Bio lead is displaced in all 50 trials at $p=5\%$ (versus 37/50 surviving bidirectional); Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead holds in only 2 of 50 trials at $p=5\%$ (versus 47/50). At $p=13\%$, directional and bidirectional converge in top-20 overlap (44\% vs.\ 47\%) because both are already near their minimum: directional lacks the bidirectional recovery at high $p$ up to this point. 
Under \emph{threshold-concentrated} perturbation, the top-20 overlap stays at or above 95\% across all tested rates including $p=80\%$, and all three field-leadership claims hold in 47--50 of 50 trials through $p=40\%$. This high stability arises because threshold-straddling errors, while in principle the most potent per author, still apply simultaneously to all competing institutions and tend to cancel within each institution's pool under bidirectional perturbation; concentrated unidirectional errors at a \emph{single} institution's threshold would be more damaging. 
Under the \emph{uniform (non-stratified)} model, top-20 overlap at $p=13\%$ rises to 87\%, and Wageningen holds rank 1 in all 50 trials (CMU in 47/50), confirming that the $3\times$ country multiplier applied to CN/KR/IR/SA institutions is the primary driver of the bidirectional scenario's instability.}
