# Response to Reviewers — Round 2

## Reviewer 1

### Comment 1.1

**Reviewer:** The institutional-attribution problem remains unresolved. The manuscript assigns each researcher’s entire career h-index to one institution—specifically, the most recent educational affiliation recorded by OpenAlex. Consequently, citations to work produced over an entire career are credited to the researcher’s latest institution, regardless of where that work was conducted. This is not a secondary limitation. It defines the institutional h2 values and, through them, the national h3 values. The response acknowledges the issue but does not test any alternative institutional-attribution method. Instead, the authors conduct a multi-field assignment analysis. That analysis addresses disciplinary classification but says nothing about whether institutional and country rankings are stable under more defensible affiliation rules. The argument that publication-level attribution is impractical does not validate the chosen approach. At minimum, the authors could examine a restricted publication window, researchers with stable affiliations, fractional treatment of multiple current affiliations, or a subset for which work-level affiliation histories are sufficiently complete. Without such evidence, the results describe the current locations of researchers carrying accumulated career h-indices, not the research performance of the institutions or countries to which those indices are assigned. This distinction is especially serious for internationally mobile and multiply affiliated researchers.


**Response:** I agree, and have implemented the reviewer's first suggestion directly: a restricted publication window, derived from the OpenAlex works snapshot rather than the per-author affiliation record. The previous rule assigned each author to their most recent educational affiliation as recorded in the author snapshot's `affiliations[].years[]` field, which tracks only which calendar years an institution appears on an author's papers — not on how many. The new rule, now the paper's default, computes a **primary institution** from per-work authorship data (OpenAlex works snapshot, `s3://openalex/data/parquet/works/`): an author's primary institution is the education institution credited on the largest number of their own authorships in the last five publishing years (the most recent publication year and the four before it). Two thresholds must be met: the institution must be credited on at least two works in the window, and on at least 10% of the window's works that credit *any* education institution. The second threshold uses education-crediting works as its denominator so that hospital- or institute-based faculty are not penalised when the paper does not list any university at all. Ties go first to the institution seen most recently, then most often across the whole career, then by smallest OpenAlex institution ID.

Affiliation *order* within a work is not used as a signal for primary-ness: a spot check of OpenAlex's `authorships[].affiliations[]` ordering against Crossref and Europe PMC found agreement on only about 49% of papers (`src/affiliation_order_check.py`), making order an unreliable proxy for primary vs. secondary affiliation.

The "rollup" credit rule (default) additionally credits the education-type ancestors of any non-education institution on an authorship. So, a Jet Propulsion Laboratory paper credits Caltech, and a Ragon Institute paper credits Harvard and MIT. Education institutions are never rolled up to their own parents (a UC Berkeley paper does not also credit the University of California System). This is the only source of "imputed" education credit; the filter thresholds are applied after rollup.

Of 126.6 million unique authors in the works snapshot, 41.8 million (33.0%) have at least one education institution credited in their five-year window; 25.9 million fail the ≥2-papers threshold and 2,326 fail the ≥10%-share threshold, leaving 15.9 million assigned. Joining with the 30.0 million qualifying authors in the snapshot yields the **13.3 million authors across 22,980 institutions** that form the basis for all rankings in this paper.

To directly address the reviewer's request to "test other attribution methods," I report a five-way sensitivity comparison in a new table in `sections/results.tex`. The four alternative variants are:

| Variant | Description |
|---|---|
| Primary + rollup (default) | 5-year window, education ancestors credited |
| Primary, no rollup | 5-year window, direct education credit only |
| Primary, share of all works | 5-year window, 10% threshold on all works |
| Multi-institution (all qualifying) | Each author credited at every qualifying institution (+29% over default) |

The multi-institution variant addresses the reviewer's request for "fractional treatment of multiple current affiliations." Since h-indices are integer-valued, crediting each author's full h₁ at every qualifying institution is the closest tractable analog to fractional credit. Of the 13.3M qualifying authors, 18.0% qualify at ≥2 institutions under this rule. Institution h₂ Spearman correlation is ρ=0.939 (7/10 top-10 overlap); country h₃ is ρ=0.995 (9/10 top-10 overlap). The three institutions entering the multi-institution top-10 that were not in the default top-10 are large European universities whose researchers commonly hold dual appointments with national research agencies (CNRS, INSERM).

**Manuscript changes:**
- Methodology, "Author-level assignment" subsection: rewritten to describe the primary-institution rule, the rollup crediting logic, the two thresholds, tie-breaking, why affiliation order is not used, and the exclusion/inclusion counts. "Robustness to institution assignment" subsection: updated to describe the variants.
- Results: sensitivity table updated to variants including multi-institution; prose added describing multi-institution results.
- Conclusion: Limitations paragraph updated

### Comment 1.2

**Reviewer:** The Monte Carlo analysis does not simulate the main data-generating errors. The new sensitivity analysis randomly selects authors and multiplies their h-indices by ratios drawn from four manually identified outliers. This design is insufficient for several reasons. First, author-disambiguation errors are unlikely to be independent and identically distributed. The manuscript itself states that they are concentrated among common names, non-Anglophone researchers, and particular countries. An independent uniform perturbation therefore removes precisely the institutional, linguistic, and geographic clustering that could create systematic ranking bias. Second, the simulation represents only upward inflation. Real disambiguation problems include both merges and splits, missing works, erroneous affiliations, duplicate profiles, and incorrect assignment of works between researchers. These processes can raise or lower an author’s h-index and need not resemble multiplication by one of four ratios. Third, the analysis adds synthetic error to a baseline already known to contain unknown error. Similarity between a perturbed ranking and the contaminated baseline is not evidence of similarity to the unknown true ranking. Fourth, the reported plateau at high perturbation rates is partly a consequence of applying similar positive inflation to competitors throughout the dataset. Such cancellation is built into the perturbation design and cannot establish robustness to concentrated or directional bias. The top-20 institutional overlap also falls to 78% at the 10% and 13% rates, which is not negligible for a paper centered on rankings. Finally, five trials per condition are too few to characterize uncertainty, particularly for the field-leadership results. The non-monotonic survival rates in Table 11—for example, leadership claims becoming more stable again at 40% or 80% error—illustrate the instability and artificial character of the exercise. The conclusion that the risks are “bounded rather than fatal” and that the rankings are robust “at every rate tested” is therefore not warranted.

**Response:** I agree with all four specific critiques and have revised the Monte Carlo design accordingly.
1. Trials per condition: All results are now averaged over 50 independent trials, up from 5, per rate (350 trials total), making the field-leadership fraction estimates meaningful.
2. Bidirectional perturbation: The perturbation now applies either upward inflation (×ratio, simulating a merged-identity error) or downward deflation (×ratio⁻¹, simulating a split-identity error), each with probability ½, drawn from the same four ratios as before. This directly addresses the reviewer's second point.
3. Country-stratified error rates: Authors affiliated with institutions in China, South Korea, Iran, or Saudi Arabia receive 3× the base error rate (reflecting the cross-validation finding that disambiguation errors concentrate in those strata). This partially addresses the iid concern for the most heavily affected strata, though it cannot model correlated errors within a single institution.
4. Cancellation effect: The reviewer notes that the plateau at high p is partly a consequence of similar inflation applying to competitors. The bidirectional design makes this explicit: at high error rates, roughly equal numbers of inflation and deflation events accumulate within every institution, partially restoring the pre-perturbation rank ordering. Top-20 institution overlap follows a U-shaped pattern: 95% at p=1%, declining to 44% near p=20%, then recovering to 83% at p=80%. I now describe this as a cancellation effect rather than evidence of robustness per se.
5. Revised claims: The "bounded rather than fatal" and "robust at every rate tested" language is removed. The paper explicitly notes that stability under this perturbation model cannot rule out concentrated or directional bias.
6. The reviewer's third point (adding synthetic error to a baseline already containing unknown error) is an irreducible limitation of any in-sample sensitivity analysis; I have not resolved it. The paper acknowledges this in its framing of the cross-validation sample as an adversarial check rather than a population-error-rate estimate.

**Manuscript changes:**
- Methodology, "Sensitivity to disambiguation error" subsection: paragraph updated to describe bidirectional perturbation, country-stratified rates (CN/KR/IR/SA at 3× base), and 50 trials per rate.
- Results: all ρ and overlap values updated from 50-trial means; field-leadership fractions updated to X/50 format; caption updated. Plateau language replaced with U-shaped cancellation-effect description; "bounded rather than fatal" and "robust at every rate tested" removed; concluding paragraph rewritten with narrower claim.

### Comment 1.3

**Reviewer:** The external validation is neither independent nor supportive of the efficiency claim. 
The manuscript describes the Leiden Ranking comparison as an “independent” validation, but the Leiden Ranking Open Edition used here is itself based on OpenAlex. The two measures therefore share source coverage, author-work links, institutional identifiers, affiliation errors, and citation data. This is convergent comparison within the same underlying data infrastructure, not independent external validation. More importantly, raw h2 correlates more strongly with MNCS (ρ = 0.634) than the proposed efficiency measure does (ρ = 0.580). These results do not demonstrate that the normalization improves the measurement of research quality or adds information beyond an existing field-normalized indicator. Statistical significance is unsurprising with 1,495 observations and is not the relevant criterion. The manuscript would need to show incremental validity—for example, through partial correlations controlling for size, comparisons of explanatory or predictive performance, or tests against genuinely external measures. The efficiency tables themselves raise additional validity concerns. Several highly ranked institutions are community colleges or other organizations for which the reported author counts and h2 values appear implausible. These cases require direct validation rather than being presented as substantive findings. They may reflect the same historical-affiliation and author-disambiguation problems that motivated the original review. Accordingly, the statement that the comparison demonstrates “genuine differences in research excellence rather than differences in institutional size” should be removed. The evidence does not support that inference.

**Response:** I agree on both counts. I removed the Leiden Open Edition comparison. I have added a new, independent comparison against Scimago Institutions Rankings (SIR) 2026, which is Scopus-derived. Joining on normalized institution name, I find the same pattern the reviewer flagged in the Leiden comparison, now confirmed against a source with no shared infrastructure with OpenAlex at all: raw h2 tracks an external composite ranking more closely than the size-normalized efficiency metric does. I no longer claim the efficiency normalization "demonstrates genuine differences in research excellence." I now report correlations as description rather than validation, and discuss directly, in a revised paragraph, why efficiency normalization apparently does not track independent external rankings as well as the raw (scale-dominated) measure, most plausibly because SIR's composite is itself influenced by scale-correlated factors (institutional prominence, resource concentration) that raw h2 partially captures and that dividing out size removes.

Regarding community colleges, the primary-institution rule implemented in response to Comment 1.1 resolves the core issue. Community colleges that appeared near the top of the efficiency table did so because authors listed them on a small number of papers (an alumni or demographic affiliation); under the new rule, a primary institution must appear on at least 2 works *and* at least 10% of the author's education-crediting works in the last five years, a threshold those handful-of-papers affiliations cannot clear. Bellevue University, Imperial Valley College, City College of San Francisco, and Frederick Community College no longer appear in the top efficiency rankings.

**Manuscript changes:**
- Methodology, Results, and Conclusion: validation against the Leiden Ranking dropped, replaced with validation against Scimago Institutions Rankings, describing the SIR data source. A synthesis paragraph noting both external comparisons show raw h2 outperforming ε2, read as description rather than validation.
- References: added SCImago Institutions Rankings 2026, Overall Rank export.

### Comment 1.4

**Reviewer:** Field-classification validity remains assumed rather than demonstrated. The manuscript cites topic-level classifier accuracy and then asserts that field-level accuracy is “substantially higher” because some topic errors remain within a parent field. This is plausible, but no field-level validation is presented, and the manuscript acknowledges that no formal evaluation is available. Plausibility should not be stated as an empirical result. The multi-field analysis tests an alternative allocation rule using the same underlying OpenAlex classifications; it does not test whether those classifications are correct. Moreover, the substantive leaders are not fully robust: only 18 of 27 field leaders remain unchanged at the 20% threshold, while the Wageningen Environmental Science and Carnegie Mellon Computer Science conclusions change under alternative specifications. These are prominent findings in the abstract and conclusion. The results therefore demonstrate meaningful specification dependence, not merely minor noise.

**Response:** I agree "substantially higher" overstates a claim I have not measured directly, and have softened it to "plausibly higher," with an added clause making explicit that this is an inference from the structure of the classification hierarchy, not a measured field-level accuracy figure. No field-level validation of the classifier is presented. I also checked whether the abstract and conclusion's claims about Wageningen's Environmental Science lead and Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead were already conditioned on the single-field assignment: Wageningen's Environmental Science lead already carried this caveat from the round-1 revision; Carnegie Mellon's Computer Science lead did not (the paper's own robustness section shows it falls to Stanford at the 20% multi-field threshold), so I added the missing qualifier.

**Manuscript changes:**
- Methodology, "Author-level assignment" subsection: "field-level accuracy is substantially higher" changed to "plausibly higher, though this is an inference from the structure of the classification hierarchy rather than a directly measured field-level accuracy figure: no field-level validation of the classifier is presented here."
- Abstract and Conclusion: added a qualifier noting the Carnegie Mellon Computer Science lead, like the Wageningen Environmental Science lead, is a single-field-assignment result that changes under the paper's own multi-field robustness check.

### Comment 1.5

**Reviewer:** The indicators remain dominated by scale, while the interpretation continues to invoke excellence. The manuscript reports that size accounts for approximately 91% of institutional h2 variation and that institution count accounts for approximately 92% of country h3 variation. This confirms that the raw indicators primarily measure scale. The proposed efficiency normalization is based on an empirical power-law relationship, but its theoretical compounding foundation fails by 177% and 97%, and the external comparison does not validate the normalization as a superior measure of quality. Despite acknowledging these problems, the manuscript continues to use expressions such as “global research strength,” “research depth,” “high-quality research systems,” and “genuine differences in research excellence.” These interpretations exceed what has been established. At most, the study documents patterns in successive h-indices computed from a particular OpenAlex snapshot under specific author, affiliation, and field-allocation rules.

**Response:** I agree, and have made the language changes throughout. "Global research strength" is replaced with "field-level h₂ rankings." "High-quality research systems" was changed to "compact research systems that place well on this metric"; remaining instances of "research depth" and "genuine differences in research excellence" are no longer present in the active manuscript text. The Conclusion's sensitivity-analysis sentence, which used the superseded "bounded rather than fatal" and "plateauing" language from the five-trial run, is updated to the 50-trial values and narrower claim: rank correlations remain high (ρ(h₂) ≥ 0.979; ρ(h₃) ≥ 0.994), UCAS Environmental Science is stable throughout, and the Wageningen and CMU field-leadership claims are sensitive above p ≈ 5%. No claim in the manuscript now exceeds what the data establish: the paper documents patterns in successive h-indices computed from a particular OpenAlex snapshot under specific author, affiliation, and field-allocation rules.

### Comment 1.6

**Reviewer:** The country rankings inherit and compound the attribution problem. The h3 calculation assigns institutional h2 values to countries using current OpenAlex institutional locations. Because each institutional h2 already attributes researchers’ accumulated career impact to their most recent institution, international mobility and secondary affiliations can reallocate historical research impact across countries. The manuscript itself identifies Saudi Arabia as an apparent example of this distortion, yet continues to interpret other country efficiency rankings substantively. Once a known artifact can elevate a country to fourth place in the efficiency ranking, the validity of the entire country comparison requires systematic investigation. It is not sufficient to label one conspicuous case as a limitation while treating the remaining rankings as genuine research-system differences.

**Response:** The primary-institution rule implemented in response to Comment 1.1 partially addresses this at the country level. Saudi Arabia's efficiency rank falls from 4th (ε3 = 6.69, old rule) to **5th (ε3 = 5.74, new rule)**: the new rule requires an institution to appear on a plurality of the author's recent works, so a secondary Saudi affiliation listed on only a few papers for funding purposes will not become the primary institution for that author. It does not fully remove the effect for researchers who genuinely list a Saudi institution on most of their recent output, the mechanism the manuscript cites, and Saudi Arabia remains an outlier.

I am not claiming the country-efficiency comparison is now systematically validated. The interpretive language around country efficiency rankings is moderated throughout (removing claims like "confirming that their research depth is genuine rather than an artefact of institutional scale"); the Saudi Arabia caveat now explicitly notes that the new rule partially suppresses this kind of distortion without eliminating it, and that Saudi Arabia is not necessarily the only country affected.

Institution→country mapping (which country an institution is located in) is a static lookup independent of researcher mobility. The reviewer's concern operates entirely at the researcher→institution attribution step.

**Manuscript changes:**
- Results, "Country efficiency" subsection: "a top tier of countries with compact but high-quality research systems" changed to "compact research systems that place well on this metric"; "confirming that their research depth is genuine rather than an artefact of institutional scale" replaced with a sentence noting that placing well on both raw h3 and efficiency is a weaker claim than confirming genuine research depth, since neither measure rules out affiliation-attribution distortion; Saudi Arabia sentence extended to note the multi-institution check rules out the tiebreak mechanism specifically without clearing other countries of comparable risk.
- Conclusion: Limitations paragraph's Saudi Arabia sentence updated: rank 4th → 5th, ε3 = 6.69 → 5.74; old "current-affiliation attribution" paragraph replaced with three new limitations; community-college caveat removed (resolved by new rule).
